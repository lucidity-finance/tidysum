# What advice costs

Tidysum exists because the usual way to hand off your money, a financial adviser or wealth
manager, usually costs a percentage of everything you have, every year. This page collects what
the industry charges, with sources, and works out what that costs over time.

Tidysum is not a substitute for advice you need from a licensed professional, and it is not a
registered investment adviser. See the [disclaimer](../DISCLAIMER.md).

## What advisers and planners charge

| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Annual retainer            | Average $6,815 a year in 2026, up 52% from $4,484 in 2023                                                                                              | Envestnet MoneyGuide study of 491 advisers, Q1 2026, as reported by [ThinkAdvisor](https://www.thinkadvisor.com/amp/2026/05/04/average-financial-planning-retainer-fee-surges-52-in-3-years) |
| Hourly, or a one-off plan  | Median $300 an hour; $3,000 for a standalone plan                                                                                                      | Kitces Research 2024, as reported by [SmartAsset](https://smartasset.com/financial-advisor/financial-advisor-cost)                                                                           |
| Robo-advisers              | About 0.25% a year at Betterment and Wealthfront, about 0.15% at Vanguard Digital Advisor, 0% at Schwab Intelligent Portfolios (with a $5,000 minimum) | [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/best/robo-advisors), October 2026                                                                                                          |

Fees change, and every firm sets its own. Check a firm's Form ADV Part 2A brochure for what it
actually charges.

## What 1% costs over time

The SEC's own example: $100,000 growing 4% a year for 20 years ends at about $208,000 with a
0.25% yearly fee and about $179,000 with a 1% fee
([Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/news-alerts/investor-bulletins/investor-bulletin-how-fees-expenses-affect-your-investment-portfolio)).

Our own arithmetic, assuming 6% a year before fees, index funds costing 0.05% a year in every
case, and no new money added:

| Starting with | Years | What a 1% adviser fee costs | Share of the ending balance | What a 0.25% robo fee costs |
| ------------- | ----- | --------------------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| $250,000      | 30    | about $351,000              | 25%                         | about $97,000               |
| $500,000      | 20    | about $274,000              | 17%                         | about $73,000               |
| $500,000      | 30    | about $701,000              | 25%                         | about $194,000              |
| $1,000,000    | 30    | about $1,402,000            | 25%                         | about $387,000              |

Saving $20,000 a year from zero, a 1% fee costs about $734,000 over 40 years, 23% of the ending
balance. In the first year alone, 1% is $2,500 on $250,000 and $10,000 on $1 million, and the bill
grows with the portfolio.

Each row is `start × (1 + 0.06 − fee − 0.0005)^years`, compared with the same formula without the
adviser's fee. Real returns vary year to year, and taxes are left out; the point is the gap, not
the ending balance.

## What you get for the fee

Many advisers do much more than pick funds: tax planning, estate work, insurance, a person to call
when markets fall. Kitces describes the typical 1% as roughly half investment management and half
financial planning. The investment half, and much of the everyday planning, follows a playbook
that is widely shared across the industry: a diversified, low-cost index-fund mix matched to your
risk, an emergency fund, the full employer match, tax-advantaged accounts first, cash earning a
real rate, debt paid down by interest rate. That part is what Tidysum and your agent take on.
